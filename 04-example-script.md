## B. Full narration script (~2,300 words)

> Formatting: one paragraph = one breath group. Punch lines sit on their own line.
> `[LABEL]` lines are spoken **and** shown on screen as section cards.

It's 4:52 on Thanksgiving afternoon, and you're standing in your aunt's kitchen holding a paper plate that's bending under the weight of too much stuffing.

The TV in the living room is loud. Somebody's kid is crying. And your Uncle Ray leans against the counter, beer in hand, looks at the work boots you didn't have time to change out of, and says it.

"So. Still fixing air conditioners?"

A couple of people laugh. Not mean. Just the kind of laugh families do when they've already decided who you are.

You smile. "Still fixing air conditioners."

Your cousin Tyler is telling everyone about the Model Y he just leased. Six hundred and forty-nine a month. He says it the way people say things they want you to ask about. Your sister got engaged. Ray's son just started at a consulting firm downtown.

And you, in their minds, are the one who didn't go to college. The one who's always a little sweaty. The one who probably needs a plate to take home.

Here's what nobody at this table knows.

You own three buildings. You own a company with four trucks and three employees. And last month, a business broker you never told anyone about put a number on that company: six hundred thousand dollars.

You are thirty-four years old, and your net worth just crossed one point three million.

Nobody here knows that. And you intend to keep it that way.

[LEVEL ONE — THE STARTING NUMBER.]

Twelve years ago, you were twenty-two, and you made seventeen dollars and fifty cents an hour as an HVAC apprentice.

That's about thirty-six thousand, four hundred dollars a year. Before taxes.

You spent that summer in attics. If you've never been in an attic in July, here's what it's like: it's a hundred and thirty degrees, the insulation sticks to your forearms, and you can feel your heartbeat in your eyes. Your boss, a quiet man named Walt, would hand you a frozen water bottle at noon and say, "Drink half. Hold the other half against your neck."

Walt was the first person who ever talked to you about money like it was a tool, not a scoreboard.

He'd been doing this for thirty-one years. Drove a fifteen-year-old Silverado. And one day, sitting in that truck eating gas station chicken, he mentioned, almost by accident, that he owned the building his ex-wife's hair salon was in. And the one next to it.

"Trade money's real money," he said. "People just spend it like it isn't."

You didn't understand what he meant yet. But you remembered it.

That year you opened a Roth IRA, mostly because Walt told you to, and you put in three hundred dollars a month. You split a two-bedroom with a guy from the supply house. Your share of rent was six-fifty.

It wasn't impressive. It wasn't supposed to be.

[LEVEL TWO — THE HOUSE THAT PAID FOR ITSELF.]

By twenty-four you had your license, and your pay jumped to fifty-eight thousand a year.

This is the moment where most people upgrade. New truck. Nicer apartment. Something to show for it.

You did something that looked, to everyone around you, like a step backwards.

You bought a duplex. An ugly one. Brown siding, a chain-link fence, a backyard that was mostly dirt. Two hundred fourteen thousand dollars.

With an FHA loan, the down payment was three and a half percent. Seven thousand, four hundred ninety dollars. Plus about six thousand two hundred in closing costs. It took nearly every dollar you'd saved outside your Roth.

You moved into one side. You rented the other side to a nurse who worked nights, for twelve hundred and fifty a month.

Your total mortgage payment, with taxes and insurance, was sixteen-twenty.

Do the math. She paid twelve-fifty. You paid three hundred seventy.

You were now living for less than your old rent, in a place you owned, while someone else paid down most of your loan every month.

Your mom came to see it once. She stood in the kitchen, looked at the linoleum peeling up in the corner, and said, "Honey, are you okay? Is money tight?"

You said, "I'm good, Mom."

You didn't explain. Explaining would have turned it into a conversation. A conversation would have turned into opinions.

[LEVEL THREE — THE BORING YEARS.]

Here's the part that never makes it into the highlight reel.

From twenty-five to twenty-eight, almost nothing happened.

You went to work. You fixed condensers and replaced capacitors and crawled under houses. You came home and fixed the things that broke in your own building, because now you could. A leaky shutoff valve. A garbage disposal. A dryer vent packed with three years of lint.

Every month, a thousand dollars went into index funds. A total market fund and an international fund. You didn't check them. You didn't trade them. You set the transfer for the day after payday, so the money left before you could think of a reason to keep it.

A thousand dollars a month is twelve thousand a year. That's not a number anyone puts on Instagram.

But it's not a number that disappears, either.

The friends you'd grown up with were starting to look more successful than you. One bought a lifted truck. One posted from Cancun twice in one year. At a birthday party, someone asked if you were "still living in that duplex," and the word *still* did a lot of work in that sentence.

You said yes.

What you didn't say was that your tenant had just signed a lease renewal at thirteen hundred, and your side of the building was now costing you three hundred and twenty dollars a month.

[LEVEL FOUR — DOING IT AGAIN.]

At twenty-eight, you did the thing that would have made Uncle Ray choke on his beer.

You moved out of your own duplex. You rented your side for thirteen-fifty. And you bought another one.

Two hundred sixty-eight thousand. Owner-occupied, five percent down. Thirteen thousand, four hundred dollars.

Now you had two buildings, four units, three tenants, and one very small bedroom with a window unit that rattled.

Your friends thought you'd moved because the old place got too run-down.

In reality, the old place had become a machine.

[LEVEL FIVE — THE YEAR IT ALMOST BROKE.]

Then twenty-nine happened.

In February, the tenant in your new building stopped paying. Not dramatically. Just silence. Texts went unanswered. The rent never came.

In March, the furnace in the first duplex died. You could fix it yourself, which saved labor, but the heat exchanger alone was over two thousand dollars.

In April, a water heater split open and flooded the laundry room.

By the time the eviction was final and the unit was cleaned and re-rented, you'd lost nine thousand, eight hundred dollars.

That number sat in your stomach for months.

You remember sitting in your truck in a Home Depot parking lot, staring at your banking app, and thinking for the first time: maybe they were right. Maybe the people buying trucks and going to Cancun were the smart ones. At least they got the trip.

This is where most people quit. Not because the math stopped working, but because the feeling did.

You didn't sell. Mostly because selling would have been a decision, and you were too tired to make one. The automatic transfer kept going. The other three units kept paying. The index funds kept doing what index funds do, which is nothing exciting, for a long time.

Fourteen months later, both buildings were full, rents were up, and the hole was gone.

Nobody ever knew it had been there.

[LEVEL SIX — COLD FRONT.]

At thirty, Walt retired.

On his last day, he handed you a binder. Inside were forty-one names. Little old ladies, a dentist's office, two churches, a laundromat. Every one of them had paid Walt for a spring and fall maintenance check for years.

"They'll need somebody," he said. "Might as well be you."

You formed an LLC that weekend. You named it Cold Front Mechanical, because it was the first thing you thought of and the filing fee was due.

You did maintenance calls on Saturdays. A hundred and eighty-nine dollars a year per customer for two visits. Forty-one customers became sixty. Sixty became a hundred and forty.

That's twenty-six thousand dollars a year in maintenance plans alone. And every maintenance visit turned into repair work about a third of the time.

At thirty-two, you quit your job.

You didn't make a speech. You gave two weeks' notice. Your boss shook your hand and said, "Starting your own thing? Good for you, kid." He assumed it was a little side gig.

By thirty-three, Cold Front had four trucks and three technicians, and it was paying you a hundred and eighteen thousand dollars a year.

At thirty-one, in the middle of all of it, you'd bought a third duplex. Three hundred and five thousand. You did most of the renovation yourself, at night, with a headlamp.

[LEVEL SEVEN — THE NUMBER NOBODY SEES.]

So here's what's actually true about the guy in work boots holding the bending paper plate.

Three duplexes. Six units. Five rented. Together they're worth a little over a million dollars, and you owe about seven hundred and ten thousand on them. Call it three hundred thousand in equity, growing every single month whether you think about it or not.

The index funds and the Roth IRA, the money you set on autopilot at twenty-two and twenty-five and never touched, are sitting at about four hundred and twelve thousand.

Cold Front Mechanical, according to the broker, is worth around six hundred thousand to the right buyer.

Add a little cash in the bank, and it's one point three six million dollars.

And here's the part that still surprises you. You never earned more than fifty-eight thousand dollars a year until you were thirty. You didn't get rich because of a big salary. You got rich because you kept your costs boring and let other people's rent, and time, do the heavy lifting.

That's the part nobody tells you about money. It isn't about how much you make. It's about how much of it you let go to work instead of letting it go to show.

[LEVEL EIGHT — THE TENANT.]

Back in the kitchen, Tyler is still talking. Except now he's complaining.

"My landlord's raising the rent fifty bucks," he says. "Some LLC. Cold Front something. Never even met the guy. It's all through a property manager."

Your fork stops halfway to your mouth.

You know exactly which unit he's talking about. The left side of the third building. You approved the application three months ago through your property manager. You saw the name. You assumed it was a different Tyler. There are a lot of Tylers.

Uncle Ray laughs. "Cold Front. Hey, isn't that the name on your truck?"

The room gets a little quieter. Or maybe it only feels that way.

You shrug. "There's like five HVAC companies with 'cold' in the name, Ray."

It's not even a lie. There are at least three.

Tyler shrugs too and goes back to talking about his car. The moment passes. But your heart is pounding in a way it never did in a hundred-and-thirty-degree attic.

That's the thing about quiet wealth. It isn't one decision. It's a thousand small decisions to not be seen, and every one of them can come undone with a single sentence at a family dinner.

[LEVEL NINE — THE COST.]

Later, after pie, your dad finds you on the back porch.

He doesn't look at you when he talks. He says the transmission on his truck is going. He says it'll be about three thousand dollars. He says he hates asking.

You could pay it a hundred times over without noticing. And for a second, you want to tell him everything. The buildings, the company, the number. You want to watch his face change. You want him to be proud in the way you've always wanted him to be proud.

Instead, you say, "I had a good month. Let me cover it."

He hugs you, a little too long, and says, "You're a good kid."

And you feel the strange weight of it. Being helped is easy to explain. Being the one who can help, quietly, without anyone knowing why, is lonelier than anyone tells you.

You think about Walt. Thirty-one years in a fifteen-year-old Silverado. Two buildings nobody knew about. You wonder now how many family dinners he sat through being the guy who fixes air conditioners.

You think he'd understand.

[THE RETURN.]

It's 4:52 on Thanksgiving afternoon again, a year later, a different paper plate, the same kitchen.

Tyler leased a different car. Ray has a new beer and the same joke.

"So. Still fixing air conditioners?"

You smile, and this time you mean it completely.

"Still fixing air conditioners."

Because you are. Some Saturdays, you still take a call yourself, just to stay sharp, just to remember the attic. The difference is that now it's a choice. Nothing in your life depends on anyone at this table believing you're doing well.

And that, it turns out, is what doing well actually feels like.

## D. Voiceover direction notes
- Overall: calm, intimate, ~158 wpm. Think "telling a friend a secret late at night".
- Uncle Ray's line: say it lightly, almost affectionate, not mocking.
- "Here's what nobody at this table knows." Drop pitch slightly, slow to ~140 wpm, then pause 1 s.
- The Level labels: neutral, slightly slower, 1 s pause after.
- Level Five (setback): heavier, slower.
- Level Eight ("Cold Front something"): tension. Shorter pauses, quieter.
- Final question: slow, warm, 2 s silence before the end screen.
- TTS number spellings are already written out in words in the script.
